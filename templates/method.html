How it works

Rules, not vibes

Every label on this site comes from a written rule applied to public data. Here is the whole recipe.

The data

  • Google Trends. Weekly worldwide search interest for each tracked term over five years, fetched one term at a time so each series is on its own 0 to 100 scale (100 is that term's busiest week). The unfinished current week is dropped.
  • Wikipedia page views. Monthly views of the matching English article, from the official Wikimedia API. A second, independent signal: when it points the same way as Google, the label is sturdier.
  • Pantone season palettes. Colour names, codes and chip colours from Pantone's New York Fashion Week Color Trend Reports, four seasons in a row.

Each refresh is saved as a dated snapshot, so the site shows the last good data even when Google blocks automated requests.

The trend label

From the weekly series:

  • Year-on-year change: mean of the last 12 weeks against the same 12 weeks a year earlier. Comparing the same weeks cancels out seasons (boots always rise in October).
  • Share of five-year high: the last 12 weeks against the busiest 12-week stretch in the series.
Rules, checked in this order
Risingup 25% or more on last year
Outdown 25% or more and under 40% of its five-year high
Fadingdown 10% or more
Emergingup 10 to 25% and still climbing (the last 12 weeks rise by more than 2% a week)
Peakingat 80% or more of its five-year high
Stableeverything else

Terms averaging under 5 on Google's scale are flagged low volume: one viral week can swing them.

Confidence. Rising, Fading and Out labels say high confidence when the label has held for at least 4 weeks and the move is strong (up 50% or more, or down 25% or more), and lower confidence otherwise or when volume is low. That is a quality indicator, not a probability. Emerging, Peaking and Stable carry no confidence level because the backtest gives no evidence for one.

How these rules were chosen (version {{ version }}). Emerging and the confidence levels were added after testing them on each term's own history: picked using data before 2025, then checked on 2025 and 2026, which they had not seen. A third idea, making labels “sticky” so they change less often, was rejected because it made them less accurate.

Colours

Nobody searches for “Arabian Spice”. Each Pantone colour is matched to the nearest of about forty everyday colour words (“rust”, “sage green”) by distance in CIELAB, a colour space built to match human perception. Search interest for “<word> outfit” during the season is compared with the same dates a year earlier. The palette summary uses HSL lightness and saturation; a colour counts as neutral below 12% saturation and as warm when its hue sits in the reds, oranges and yellows.

What this does not show. The labels capture momentum that usually lasts a few more months; they do not predict which new item will break out next. Weekly observations of the same term overlap, so they are not independent, and the term list was chosen in 2026 with hindsight, which flatters how often terms keep rising.

Limits

  • Search interest measures curiosity, not sales. A term can be searched because people are mocking it.
  • Google Trends numbers are sampled and rounded, so small terms are noisy. Some weeks show the same short spike across unrelated terms (mid-February 2026 is one); comparing 12-week averages keeps a single odd week from deciding a label.
  • The term list is curated by hand and worldwide only; it is easy to edit in data/dictionary.json.
  • pytrends is an unofficial, archived library. If Google changes its site, refreshes will fail and the last snapshot stays up.
